## Key Idea

QUIC encrypts most of its header, and active scans only show what servers advertise, so it's hard to see real deployments from the outside. Mücke et al. take a clever shortcut: they look at backscatter hitting the UCSD network telescope, meaning QUIC servers replying to attack traffic spoofed with the telescope's addresses. Over about five years since standardization, these replies show that Meta, Google, and Cloudflare pick noticeably different retransmission timeouts and retry limits. Connection IDs and packet features can also fingerprint off-net servers and sometimes even reveal how many servers sit behind a load balancer.

## Critique

It's cheap, non-intrusive, and the irony is great: QUIC hides metadata, but load balancers need routable connection IDs, and those leak infrastructure. The catch is that backscatter only shows up where attackers aim, so you mostly see a few hypergiants and not much about smaller servers or clients. It tells us how the big players configure QUIC, not really how QUIC spreads across the Internet.

## Connections

[Langley et al.](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3098822.3098842) encrypted headers to avoid ossification, and whatever's left visible becomes the thing people measure. Like the partial reachability paper we first read, it squeezes new insight out of passive data that was already sitting around.
